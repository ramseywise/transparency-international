# Business and fossil fuel presence in government delegations at COP31

## Research proposal

Draft v1, 24 November 2026. For review by the lead researcher.

**Status of this draft.** Sections 3 and 4 are provisional. They rest on 15 of the 86 sources screened in so far; no formal literature review has been written yet. Sources still unread include the report that holds the counting method behind several figures cited below ("Behind the Badge"), nine articles, books and OECD reports that could not be obtained automatically, and 33 titles found in a later search. Statements in sections 3 and 4 may change when these are read. Sections 5 to 8 rely in part on sections 3 and 4 and may change with them.

This proposal contains no results. It gives no count of business or fossil fuel-linked delegates at COP31 and names no organisation as fossil fuel-linked.

## 1. Summary

This proposal sets out how the two questions in the research brief would be answered from the public list of registered participants at COP31: how many delegates in the delegations of the European Union and its 27 Member States come from business, and from which sectors; and how many come from organisations linked to the fossil fuel industry, in three tiers.

The list holds 34,809 registrations on Party and Party overflow badges. Of these, 3,821 were nominated by the EU or a Member State, and they name about 1,500 distinct home organisations. Affiliations are self-declared free text. None carries an organisation type.

The sources read so far show that earlier counts exist, including Transparency International's own for COP30, but that they differ in what they count. Published definitions of a "fossil fuel lobbyist" turn on assumed intent; the tiers in the brief turn on the sector of the employer. Two counts for the same conference, made by different organisations under different definitions, report 84 and 63 delegates brought by EU governments. The published sources do not allow the difference to be attributed to the definition alone. No source read gives working criteria for "business" or a scheme of sectors, and none reports how accurate its classification was. Sections 3 and 4 are provisional on these points.

The proposed method classifies organisations, not people. Each organisation receives a category, a short reasoning, a source with a quotation, and a confidence level defined by the strength of the evidence. A language model carries out the look-up, because roughly 1,500 organisations cannot be researched by hand in the time available. Its output is therefore built to be audited: after the run, an expert labels a random sample of about 250 organisations without seeing the model's answer and reviews every organisation the model places in a fossil fuel tier, and the published figures carry the error rates the sample shows. Organisations that cannot be classified are reported as unknown, with the reason, and never guessed.

Eighteen decisions are requested in section 9. The most consequential are the scheme of sectors, the evidence required for tiers 2 and 3, the treatment of trade associations and state-owned enterprises, the accuracy the classification must reach before its figures are used, and whether individual delegates with no stated organisation may be looked up on public pages.

No classification starts until the lead researcher has replied to this proposal.

## 2. Background and research questions

The brief is the document that commissions this study. It is not a published source, so quotations from it carry no endnote. The purpose, in its words:

> "This research should measure how much of the government-nominated presence at COP31 (UN climate conference 2026) comes from business, and how much of that is linked to the fossil fuel industry. Governments decide who gets the conference badges, with no UN eligibility rules or screening, so they are a key route for industry access to the negotiations."

The brief distinguishes two badge types. A Party badge is "the official national delegation, normally officials, negotiators and ministers". A Party overflow badge is one of the "extra badges a government hands out at its own discretion, often to business, NGOs, academics and guests. Holders get access to the Blue Zone (the official, UN-run conference area), and can observe negotiating rooms at times."

**Question 1: "How many delegates come from business, and from which sectors?"**

For the EU and each of its 27 Member States, the brief asks for a count of Party and Party overflow delegates whose home organisation is a business, by sector, with the two badge types shown separately, as a number and as a share of each delegation. Across all countries, "we could repeat the count if feasible", with priority to business on full Party badges.

The brief defines business as "for-profit companies, industry and trade associations, consultancies, law firms, and financial institutions". It adds: "Could be interesting to count state-owned enterprises separately rather than as public sector. Exclude NGOs, universities, think tanks and media."

**Question 2: "How many fossil fuel-linked delegates did EU countries invite?"**

The brief sets three tiers:

- Tier 1 (direct fossil fuel interests): oil, gas and petrochemical companies, as well as fossil fuel infrastructure operators.
- Tier 2 (partial fossil fuel exposure): utilities and companies operating in sectors with ongoing fossil fuel activities or dependencies.
- Tier 3 (financial exposure): banks, insurers and asset managers with documented fossil fuel financing exposure.

The wording of both questions is kept as given. Section 9 lists every point at which this proposal would narrow or extend it.

## 3. What the literature says (provisional)

### 3.1 What has been read

Fifteen sources have been read in full: the three named in the brief and twelve of the documents they cite. They include one peer-reviewed article,[3] reports, articles and press releases by Transparency International and other civil society organisations, a recommendation of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD),[16] and journalism. Each is identified in the endnotes. Annex B gives the counts for the search as a whole.

### 3.2 How Party and Party overflow badges work

Parties are "nation states, plus the European Union, that have ratified the convention and play a full part in negotiations".[1] Before COP28, overflow participants could be nominated "without their names appearing on their official lists"; in the COP28 and COP29 lists they are named.[2] The COP28 list was the first to be released as a spreadsheet giving the nominating body, the affiliation and the badge type of each participant.[3] A rule requiring registrants to identify their affiliation took effect at COP28.[4]

Overflow delegates "cannot negotiate or speak on a country's behalf".[5] They are nonetheless "officially part of the state delegation".[6] According to Transparency International's analysis of COP30, they are not subject to the disclosure requirements that apply to observer organisations.[7]

Two points bear on how a delegation count should be read. First, Party badges themselves are not reserved for officials: they have been given to civil society, youth and private sector representatives,[8] and "some countries allocate some of their party badges to NGOs, which can artificially inflate the size of their official delegation".[9] Second, at COP30 "more than 95% of non-state actors who entered through EU Member State delegations did so on Party Overflow badges".[10] The brief's request to report the two badge types separately is consistent with both.

### 3.3 Earlier counts of business presence

Only one source read counts business delegates inside EU delegations. Transparency International's analysis of COP30 sorted delegates of the EU and its Member States into five categories by self-declared organisation, "using AI assistance": public sector; business and commercial; civil society and other non-state; media and support staff; others.[11] It reports 885 non-state delegates, of whom 526 were business and commercial and 359 civil society and other.[12] The article gives no criteria for the business category, no sectors within it, and no measure of how accurate the sorting was.

Other sources touch business presence without counting it in delegations. An earlier Transparency International article, written with InfluenceMap, found 466 companies and industry associations at COP28 or COP29 that are listed in InfluenceMap's database of corporate climate policy engagement; it does not describe how attendees were identified or sectors assigned.[13] InfluenceMap's own briefing on European industry labels 25 industry associations by sector (for example Energy, Chemicals, Cross-sector, Metals & Mining and Utilities) without stating the rule, and covers non-financial companies only.[14] Transparency International EU's analysis of lobby meetings states no rule for sorting organisations; it remarks of one body that it "labels itself an NGO", which shows that self-description is not taken at face value there.[15] The OECD recommendation on lobbying lists the kinds of actor concerned (companies, business and trade associations, non-governmental organisations, think tanks and others) but does not define business.[16]

**Finding.** No source read so far gives observable criteria for deciding that an organisation is a business, or a scheme of sectors that could be applied to a participant list.

### 3.4 Earlier counts of fossil fuel-linked participants

Several of the sources read count fossil fuel-linked participants or report such counts.[17][22][23][25][27] They use two different kinds of definition.

*Definitions based on assumed intent.* The Kick Big Polluters Out coalition counted at least 2,456 "fossil fuel lobbyists" at COP28 and at least 1,773 at COP29.[17] Its definition covers a delegate who "can be reasonably assumed" to have the objective of influencing policy "in the interests of the fossil fuel industry, or a particular fossil fuel company and its shareholders", and extends to members of a delegation.[18] A "fossil fuel company" is one with significant business activities in the exploration, extraction, refining, trading or specialised transportation of fossil fuels, or the sale of electricity derived from them; "significant" means that the company publicly declares the commercial involvement, or publicly promotes that it has significant investments in such companies.[19] A trade association is included if any one of three conditions holds: fossil fuel companies are significantly involved in its governance, its stated purpose is to serve them, or it has a recent record of lobbying in their favour.[20] Finance, agribusiness and transport are excluded.[21] Fossil Free Politics, which states its definition of a fossil fuel lobbyist in the same words, counted 84 "fossil fuel lobbyists" brought to COP30 by nine EU governments.[22]

*Definitions based on the employer's sector.* Transparency International's analysis of COP30 used the three tiers repeated in the brief, and counted 63 delegates across nine EU delegations, after consolidating duplicates and subsidiaries into 21 entities.[23] It states that "the analysis deliberately excluded fossil fuel-intensive sectors such as steel and shipping".[24]

The peer-reviewed study, of COP28, did not classify delegates itself: it took its 2,347 fossil fuel delegates (3 per cent of 69,999 on-site participants) from the Kick Big Polluters Out list, and checked it by independently identifying delegates of major oil and gas companies.[25]

These approaches differ on points that the brief's tiers leave open:

| Point | Intent-based counts | Transparency International, COP30[23] |
|---|---|---|
| Electricity utilities | inside "fossil fuel company" where power is sold from fossil fuels[19] | tier 2 |
| Banks, insurers, asset managers | excluded[21] | tier 3 |
| Trade associations | three stated criteria, any one of which suffices[20] | not addressed in the tiers |
| Steel, shipping and similar sectors | not addressed | deliberately excluded[24] |
| Subsidiaries | not addressed | consolidated with the parent; no rule stated[23] |
| Threshold for "partial exposure" or "documented financing" | not applicable | none stated |

For COP30 and EU governments, one source reports 84 and the other 63.[22][23] The two figures are not directly comparable: the definitions differ, the counts were made by different organisations, and the sources read do not establish that the same delegations are covered. The difference cannot be attributed to the definition alone. The figures suggest, without showing, how far a count may depend on the definition chosen.

Three further points recur in the sources.

- **State-owned companies.** Transparency International's 2025 report defines "fossil fuel interests" as people, organisations and companies, whether private, state-owned or of mixed ownership, connected to the fossil fuel industry.[26] It reports from "Behind the Badge" that 74 per cent of the "fossil fuel lobbyists" holding Party badges at COP29 were affiliated with state-owned or partly state-owned companies.[27] No source read defines a state-owned company.
- **Trade associations.** InfluenceMap describes a "lowest common denominator effect" in which associations "are drawn towards the positions of a small proportion of their members (often from the fossil fuel value chain)", and finds some cross-sector business federations among "the most negative voices on climate policy in the region".[28] This is evidence about positions, not about membership, and gives no rule for assigning a tier.
- **Advisers and consultancies.** Ties to high-emitting industries include "paid and unpaid roles in high-emitting companies, trade associations, consultancy firms contracted by such industries".[29] A badge does not show such a tie.

**Finding.** The sources read give no threshold for tier 2 or tier 3, no rule for subsidiaries, and one explicit test for trade associations. No published list of fossil fuel-linked companies or financiers has yet been read; several are named in the sources and are on the reading list.

### 3.5 How earlier counts were made, and their stated limits

The Kick Big Polluters Out count for COP28 compared the participant list with three earlier lists by script and then checked it line by line by hand, using open sources and InfluenceMap data; delegates with no affiliation were not classified.[30] For COP29 the coalition cleaned and de-duplicated the provisional list, matched it against its earlier lists and external registers, ran a web search for each organisation, passed the result to a large language model that gave a judgement with an explanation and references, and then fact-checked by hand.[31] Transparency International's COP30 analysis also used "AI assistance". It reports that 12 of its 21 entities had been assessed by LobbyMap, InfluenceMap's platform; that assessment supplied information on policy positions and was not used to classify.[32]

None of these sources reports an accuracy figure, a validation sample or a published set of coding rules. The Kick Big Polluters Out release for COP28 links to its list; the coalition's COP29 list, the Fossil Free Politics list and the data of the peer-reviewed study are available on request.[33]

The limits the authors state themselves are these:

- The counts are described as conservative.[34]
- A count may be too high where a company is in transition, and too low where affiliations are incomplete or ties are hidden.[35]
- In the COP30 analysis, seven delegates could not be confidently classified.[36]
- At COP29, nearly 15 per cent of participants (over 6,200) did not disclose an affiliation, more than 90 per cent of them on Party or Party overflow badges, and around 10 per cent of Party badge holders were listed as unpaid advisers.[37]
- Presence is not influence. "Their presence at COP doesn't prove they swung a vote."[38] In the context of EU lobbying: "access does not equal legislative impact."[39]

### 3.6 Conflict of interest and disclosure

Three sources state that the UN climate convention has no agreed definition of a conflict of interest and no conflict-of-interest policy for those who attend.[40] A joint civil society submission of 2022, endorsed by Transparency International among 126 organisations, proposes a definition and argues that the risk differs by actor: it contrasts a trade union representing workers in a fossil fuel company with an executive of a polluting corporation.[41] The OECD recommendation treats a "delegation to an international forum" as a kind of advisory or expert group and recommends disclosure of members' private interests and professional affiliations.[42] It is not legally binding.

These sources frame why the count matters. They do not supply categories for it.

### 3.7 Gaps

On the reading so far:

1. No operational definition of "business" and no scheme of sectors (3.3).
2. No threshold for partial fossil fuel exposure or for documented financing; no rule for subsidiaries; no definition of a state-owned company (3.4).
3. No reported accuracy for any earlier classification, including those that used a language model (3.5).
4. A search of one catalogue of scholarly work found no study, judged by title, that counts business delegates inside EU Party delegations or sorts them by sector (Annex B).

Not yet read, and likely to bear on these gaps: "Behind the Badge"; the published methodology of InfluenceMap and the list of fossil fuel-linked entities attached to the COP30 analysis; academic work on conflicts of interest in the climate convention and on interest-group access in the EU; and eleven titles on the reliability of language-model classification against human coders.

## 4. Proposed definitions (provisional)

Each definition below states what would be observed, whether it is taken from a source or is this study's own proposal, and the alternatives. The working rules, examples and hard cases are written after this proposal is agreed.

### 4.1 Unit and categories

The unit classified is the home organisation; delegates are counted through it. Every organisation receives one top-level category. This proposal recommends the five categories of the COP30 analysis, for comparability with it: public sector; business and commercial; civil society and other non-state; media and support staff; others.[11] The brief's exclusions (NGOs, universities, think tanks and media) fall outside business in this scheme. Because the COP30 analysis gives no criteria, comparison with it is possible only at the level of the labels. "Support staff" and "others" describe a delegate's role and not a kind of organisation; how such entries are assigned is part of decision 1. *Status: taken from a source that gives the categories but not their criteria; the criteria are this study's own proposal.*

### 4.2 Business

*Definition (the brief's):* for-profit companies, industry and trade associations, consultancies, law firms and financial institutions.

*Proposed observable indicators (own proposal):* a company register entry or legal form showing a for-profit entity; or an association whose members are companies, as stated in its own statutes or membership page; or a description of the organisation as a company, firm or bank by an official register or by the organisation itself.

*Business types (own proposal):* company; trade or industry association; consultancy; law firm; financial institution; state-owned enterprise (see 4.4).

*Alternatives:* (a) rely on the organisation's self-description alone, which is cheaper and less reliable; (b) restrict business to companies and treat associations separately, which would depart from the brief's definition and may make the figure harder to compare with the COP30 analysis, whose business category is not defined.[11]

*Cases the indicators do not settle:* development agencies and public development banks; chambers of commerce; cooperatives; foundations set up by companies; utilities owned by a municipality; self-employed consultants; and trade unions. Each is ruled on in the classification rules, with an example, and those rules are sent for approval. Where the only evidence is the organisation's description of itself, the record carries a lower confidence level (4.6).

### 4.3 Sector

No source read supplies a scheme (3.3). Three options are put for decision in section 9:

- (a) a short list written for this study, of about ten to fifteen sectors, with energy split so that fossil fuel, electricity and renewable activities are visible;
- (b) the sector labels InfluenceMap applies to associations and companies,[14] once its methodology has been read;
- (c) an established statistical classification of economic activities, grouped into broad sections.

*Recommendation:* (a), mapped to (c) where possible so that others can re-group the results. *Status: own proposal.*

### 4.4 State-owned enterprise

*Proposed definition (own proposal):* a commercial entity in which a state holds a controlling interest, as stated by an official source or the entity's own reporting. The brief suggests counting these separately. The sources support separate reporting: state ownership is common among the fossil fuel-linked organisations on Party badges,[27] and Transparency International's own definition of fossil fuel interests includes state-owned and mixed-ownership companies.[26] No source read gives an ownership threshold. This proposal suggests that "controlling interest" mean more than 50 per cent of voting rights held by a state, directly or through public bodies; the threshold is a decision requested in section 9.

### 4.5 Fossil fuel tiers

The tier is a property of the organisation. An organisation that is not a business can still hold a tier only if the lead researcher so decides (section 9); the default proposed is that tiers apply to business organisations and state-owned enterprises.

| Tier | Definition (the brief's) | Proposed observable indicators | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Oil, gas and petrochemical companies, and fossil fuel infrastructure operators | The organisation, or its own annual reporting, states that it explores for, extracts, refines, trades or transports oil, gas or coal, produces petrochemicals, or operates pipelines, terminals or storage for them | Indicators adapted from the activity list in an earlier count[19]; adding coal is this study's proposal, since the brief's wording names oil and gas only |
| 2 | Utilities and companies in sectors with ongoing fossil fuel activities or dependencies | An electricity or heat utility that generates or sells power from fossil fuels; other sectors only if named in an agreed list | Own proposal; no threshold found in the literature |
| 3 | Banks, insurers and asset managers with documented fossil fuel financing exposure | The institution appears in a published list of fossil fuel financiers that the lead researcher has approved | Own proposal; the lists have not yet been reviewed |

Points on which the sources differ, each put for decision in section 9:

- **Utilities.** One earlier count places sellers of fossil-derived electricity inside "fossil fuel company";[19] the brief places utilities in tier 2. This proposal keeps the brief's placement.
- **Sectors with "dependencies".** The COP30 analysis excluded steel and shipping.[24] The brief's wording could include them. Without an agreed list, tier 2 has no boundary.
- **Trade associations.** The only explicit test read has three alternative criteria: governance, stated purpose and lobbying record.[20] Options: apply that test; give an association the tier of the sector it represents; or report associations as a separate line without a tier.
- **Subsidiaries.** The COP30 analysis consolidated subsidiaries with their parents without stating a rule.[23] Options: a subsidiary takes its parent's tier; or it is classified on its own activity, with the parent recorded.
- **Consultancies and law firms with fossil fuel clients.** The sources note such ties[29] but give no test. This proposal recommends that they receive no tier, because client relationships cannot be observed consistently from public sources, and that this is stated as a limitation.
- **How much activity is enough.** The indicators above do not say what share of an organisation's business must be fossil fuel-related. This proposal suggests that any current activity of the listed kinds, stated by the organisation or an official source, qualifies, because shares of revenue cannot be observed consistently across about 1,500 organisations. The alternative is a threshold, applied only where figures are published. Put for decision with the tier 2 boundary.

### 4.6 Unknown, and confidence

*Unknown* is a result, not a failure, and always carries a reason: the field is blank; the text is generic (for example "Private sector"); the text is not an organisation; the name could refer to several bodies; nothing was found; sources conflict; or the agreed rules do not cover the case. The five reasons recorded when the data were prepared (section 5) fall under the first three; the other four can arise only during classification.

*Confidence* is defined by the evidence and not by the classifier's impression. High: an official or authoritative source states what the organisation does, and the match between the name on the list and that organisation is certain. Medium: one indirect source, or some doubt about the match. Low: inferred from the name alone or from a weak source. *Status: own proposal.*

## 5. Data

The source is the list of registered on-site participants at COP31 published by the UN climate secretariat as a spreadsheet, with one sheet per badge type. The file is kept unaltered. The brief notes that "affiliations are self-declared and free text. They are often misspelt, abbreviated, generic ("Private sector") or blank."

The two sheets in scope hold 34,809 registrations: 18,812 on Party badges and 15,997 on Party overflow badges. The EU scope is fixed as 28 nominating bodies, the 27 Member States and the European Union; all 28 appear in the file. They account for 3,821 registrations: 1,381 Party and 2,440 Party overflow.

Delegations differ greatly in size and make-up. The largest nominated 607 delegates, the smallest 15. The European Union's own delegation numbers 271 (104 Party, 167 Party overflow). One Member State has no overflow delegates and another has one. Shares for small delegations will therefore move sharply with a single organisation, and are reported with their counts.

Of the 3,821 registrations, 3,799 name a home organisation. The remaining 22 do not: the field is blank (4), a placeholder (4), a generic phrase (4), the name of a country (3), or a person's name or title (7).

The 3,799 entries were typed in 1,599 distinct ways. After removal of differences in case, accents, punctuation and legal form, and the merging of six near-identical pairs, they reduce to 1,501 organisations. Of these, 840 have a single delegate. A further 108 pairs of similar names within one delegation have been set aside for a person to review and are not merged. 122 organisations are named only by something that looks like an acronym; they cover 390 delegates and will need the nominating country as context to resolve.

No person appears on both the Party and the Party overflow sheet for the same nominating body. Two rows on one sheet appear to share a person; the match is by name, so these are probable duplicates and not certain ones. Row counts before and after preparation reconcile exactly.

The list also gives each delegate's functional title, department and relation to the nominating government; the brief's examples of the last are "paid contract, partner/sponsor, guest, unpaid adviser, chose not to disclose". This proposal does not use these fields to classify organisations. Functional title and department would be used only in looking up a delegate with no stated organisation, if that is agreed (6.3). Whether results are also reported by relation is a decision requested in section 9.

The list records who registered, not who attended.

## 6. Method

### 6.1 Preparing the data

Preparation is done by code, with each rule named and counted in a log, and can be repeated from the original file. Original values are never overwritten: a corrected or inferred value sits in a separate column with its method and source. Spellings are merged automatically only when they are identical after clean-up or nearly so; anything less certain is proposed for review.

### 6.2 Classifying organisations

Classification is per organisation and is then joined back to delegates. Each organisation receives one record containing:

- the category and, where they apply, the business type, sector and fossil fuel tier;
- two or three sentences of reasoning that name the rule applied;
- the source: a web address, the quotation that supports the label, and the date it was retrieved;
- the real-world body the name was matched to;
- the confidence level (4.6).

The cheapest reliable step is taken first: fixed rules for evident government bodies; then matching against any published list the lead researcher approves; then a look-up on the web by a language model, using the organisation's own site, official registers and third-party sources. Where no source is found, no label is given.

A pilot of 50 organisations precedes the full run. The 50 are drawn at random, and the draw is recorded so that it can be repeated. The pilot measures cost, time and the share of unknowns, and tests whether the rules can be applied as written. It gives a first indication of accuracy only if the expert labels the same 50 without seeing the model's answers (section 9). The pilot organisations are not drawn again for the expert sample. The rules may be revised after the pilot and are then fixed, with a version number, before the full run. Any later change is logged with its reason.

### 6.3 Delegates with no stated organisation

For the 22 registrations without a usable organisation, and for any found to be generic during classification, two options exist: report them as unknown; or search the person's name, title and country on public pages to find the employer, recording only the employer and the source. The second would not use any page behind a log-in. This is a decision for Transparency International (section 9), which should confirm that it fits its data protection practice. An employer would be recorded only where two details on the page match the registration, for example the name and the title. Every affiliation found this way would be checked by a person, held separately, and results shown with and without it. A delegate's name would be used for this look-up and for nothing else.

### 6.4 Checks

- *Automatic, on every record:* the web address opens; the quotation appears on the page; the page concerns the matched organisation. A record that fails is run again or set to unknown.
- *Repeatability:* a random 10 per cent of organisations is classified a second time, in a fresh run that does not see the first. Agreement is reported separately for the category, the sector and the tier, as a simple rate and as a rate corrected for agreement by chance. Organisations on which the two runs disagree are added to the expert's work.
- *Known answers:* an organisation on an approved published list must come out in the corresponding tier; any miss is investigated. Because the same lists are used to classify (6.2), this shows that the matching works and is not evidence of accuracy. If more than one list is approved, one can be held back from classification and used as a test.

These checks detect errors of process. They do not measure accuracy, because they use the same kind of tool that produced the label.

### 6.5 Expert sample

Accuracy is measured by a person. The expert's work has two parts.

*Every organisation the model places in a fossil fuel tier* is reviewed by the expert. Tiers are expected to be rare, and a random sample of 250 would hold too few of them to measure anything. If their number proves larger than the expert can review, a random sample of them is taken and the report says so.

*A random sample of about 250 organisations* is drawn from all EU organisations, including those classified by fixed rules as government bodies, with the draw recorded so that it can be repeated. The sample is drawn in groups defined by the model's category and confidence level, with a minimum of 30 organisations in each group that has that many. It takes more than its share of low-confidence records, and it includes organisations predicted to be neither business nor fossil fuel-linked, which is the only way to estimate what was missed.

The expert sees the organisation, the nominating country and the evidence, but not the label or the reasoning, and records an independent label. Disagreements are then reviewed with the reasoning visible. The evidence shown is the evidence the model chose, so the expert is not fully independent of it. For a smaller part of the sample, proposed as 30 organisations, the expert is given only the name and country and looks the organisation up unaided.

The results are counted in delegates, while the sample is drawn in organisations and deliberately unevenly. Each sampled organisation is therefore weighted by how likely it was to be drawn and by its number of delegates, so that the error rates apply to the delegate counts that are published. Accuracy is reported both per organisation and per delegate.

From the sample the study reports: accuracy at each confidence level; for business and for each tier, the share of positive labels that were correct and the share of true cases that were found, each with a range of uncertainty; and a table of which categories were confused with which. The sample is too small to measure error for each delegation separately. Differences between delegations that are smaller than the measured range of error are therefore not presented as a ranking.

Thresholds for acceptable accuracy are agreed before the full run; candidate values are put for decision in section 9. If a threshold is missed, the rules or the model are changed for the whole run, the run is repeated, and accuracy is measured on a fresh sample. Individual labels the expert corrects are corrected in the table, each correction is logged, and the effect of the corrections on the counts is stated.

Independently of the sample, every organisation named in the final publication as fossil fuel-linked is checked by a person.

### 6.6 Reporting

Every table gives the count and the share, with Party and Party overflow separately, and with unknowns as their own row, broken down by reason and by delegation. Headline figures are shown with the range of error measured in the expert sample.

Each share is defined as follows:

| Share | Counted | Out of |
|---|---|---|
| Business share of a delegation | delegates whose organisation is classified as business | all registrations nominated by that body on that badge type, including those whose organisation is unknown |
| Fossil fuel-linked share of a delegation | delegates whose organisation holds a tier | the same |
| Fossil fuel-linked share of business | delegates whose organisation is business and holds a tier | delegates whose organisation is classified as business |

Unknowns stay in the total. Each share is also given as a range: the lower figure treats every unknown as outside the category, the upper figure treats every unknown as inside it. Which total is used for the headline figure is a decision requested in section 9.

Results are shown under narrower and wider definitions: tier 1 only; tiers 1 and 2; tiers 1 to 3; and with and without affiliations found by look-up. Every figure in the publication is produced by code from the classified table, which is kept with its evidence so that the result can be audited.

Question 1 for all countries is not committed here. This narrows the brief and is put for decision in section 9. The proposal is that feasibility is decided after the pilot, on its measured cost and, if the expert labels the pilot, its accuracy. An all-countries count would need its own expert sample.

## 7. Use of a language model

A language model is used at three points: reading each source into a structured record with quotations and page numbers; looking up and classifying each organisation; and drafting text from the tables and the agreed rules. Counting, name matching, sampling and statistics are done by ordinary code, which gives the same answer every time.

The reason for using a model in classification is scale: roughly 1,500 organisations, most with a single delegate, no organisation type in the data, and no labelled list from an earlier conference. The earlier counts closest to this study also used one (3.5). None reported its accuracy. This study treats the model as a research assistant whose work is sampled and measured, and not as an authority.

The known risks, and the control for each:

| Risk | What it would look like here | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Invented facts or sources | A plausible description of a company, or a web address that does not exist | No label without a source; every address and quotation is checked automatically |
| Wrong organisation | A short name matched to a different company of the same name | The nominating country is used as context; the matched body is recorded and checked |
| Confidence that means nothing | "High" given to everything | Confidence is defined by the kind of evidence, and each level is tested against the expert sample |
| Uneven coverage | Large, English-language companies are found; small or non-English ones become unknown, which biases comparison between countries | The rate of unknowns is reported for every delegation; searches are made in the country's language |
| Out-of-date knowledge | A company has merged, sold its gas business or changed its name | Nothing is taken from the model's memory; every source carries its retrieval date |
| The model's own definitions | Where a rule is vague, the model applies its own idea of "fossil fuel-linked" | Written rules with indicators and hard cases; the reasoning must name the rule; uncovered cases become unknown |
| Self-description | A company describes itself as a leader of the energy transition | For fossil fuel tiers, third-party sources rank above an organisation's account of itself |
| Leading the question | Instructions that expect to find fossil fuel links find them | Neutral instructions; the expert sample includes predicted negatives |
| Instructions hidden in web pages | A page contains text telling the model how to classify | Web content is treated as material to read, never as instruction; odd results are caught by the automatic and repeat checks |
| A different answer on each run | Results change between runs or after the model is updated | One model version for the whole run, recorded with every result; results are stored; the repeat check measures the variation |
| Checking the model with the model | Automatic checks pass and the label is still wrong | Only the expert sample counts as a measure of accuracy |
| The expert defers to the model | Seeing the label, the expert agrees | The expert labels blind first |
| Wrongly naming an organisation | A public statement that a company is fossil fuel-linked, on weak evidence | Every named organisation is checked by a person before publication |
| Under-counting | Missed fossil fuel-linked delegates make the finding too small | The share missed is estimated from the predicted negatives in the sample |
| Personal data | Looking up named delegates | Organisation first; a person is looked up only where the organisation is missing, on public pages only, if Transparency International agrees (section 9) |
| Invented or misread literature | A citation that does not exist, or a finding the source does not make | Every statement from a source carries a quotation and page; quotations are checked against the source text |

What these controls cannot remove is set out in section 8.

## 8. Limitations

- **Registered is not attended.** The list shows who was registered.
- **Affiliations are self-declared.** An organisation is classified as stated. A delegate may name an employer loosely, name a body other than the one whose interests they represent, or omit a second role. Advisers, consultants and lawyers acting for fossil fuel clients are not identified (4.5).
- **Presence is not influence.** The study measures who was given a badge by a government. It cannot show what any delegate did or whether any outcome changed.[38]
- **The tier describes the organisation, not the person.** A delegate from a tier 2 utility may work on renewable energy.
- **Definitions affect the count.** Section 3.4 gives two figures for the same conference made under different definitions. Results under narrower and wider definitions are published together for this reason, and comparison with earlier counts, including the COP30 analysis, is possible only at the level of the labels.
- **Governments fill in the form differently.** A difference between two delegations may reflect how each government registers its guests and describes their organisations, and not who it invited.
- **State-owned enterprises may drive differences between countries.** Where energy companies are publicly owned, a delegation's fossil fuel-linked count depends on how such enterprises are treated (section 9).
- **Unknowns can change the order of delegations.** Shares are published as ranges for this reason, and error is not measured for each delegation separately (6.5).
- **Published lists are incomplete.** Tier 3 rests on lists of fossil fuel financiers. An institution absent from the approved lists receives no tier, whatever its lending.
- **One expert, one sample.** Accuracy is an estimate with a range. For rare categories, such as tier 3 in a small delegation, the range will be wide. There is no measure of agreement between two human raters unless a second person labels part of the sample.
- **Name matching is imperfect.** Spellings of one body in different languages may remain separate, and two people with the same name cannot be told apart.
- **The literature base is provisional.** Sections 3 and 4 rest on 15 sources. The search covered references cited by the three starting sources and one catalogue of scholarly work. The scope so far is sources in English, although the catalogue search itself was not limited by language, and relevance was judged from titles; reports by non-governmental organisations are unlikely to be well covered by such a catalogue, and no separate search for them has been made yet (Annex B).
- **A single conference.** The study does not show change over time.

## 9. Decisions requested

Each item gives the question, the options, a recommendation with its reason, and what the choice changes later. Recommendations are this study's proposals and are marked as such.

**1. Top-level categories.** Options: (a) the five categories of the COP30 analysis; (b) a different set. *Recommendation: (a)*, for comparability, with "support staff" and "others" entries assigned by the organisation named and not by the delegate's role. Changes: the rows of every table.

**2. Scheme of sectors.** Options: (a) a short list written for this study; (b) InfluenceMap's labels; (c) a statistical classification. *Recommendation: (a), mapped to (c).* The literature gives no answer. Changes: the breakdown for Question 1 and the comparability of the results.

**3. State-owned enterprises.** Options: (a) a separate business type, reported on its own line and included in business totals; (b) separate and outside business totals; (c) public sector. Also: the level of state ownership that qualifies. *Recommendation: (a), with more than 50 per cent of voting rights as the test.* Changes: the size of the business count, particularly in the fossil fuel tiers.

**4. Coal.** The brief's tier 1 names oil, gas and petrochemicals. *Recommendation: include coal companies in tier 1.* Changes: tier 1 counts.

**5. Tier 2 boundary.** Options: (a) electricity and heat utilities with fossil fuel generation only; (b) those plus an agreed list of sectors (the brief's "dependencies"), on which steel, shipping, aviation, cement and others would need a decision one by one. *Recommendation: (a) as the headline, and (b) as a wider variant if the lead researcher supplies or approves the list.* The COP30 analysis excluded steel and shipping. Also for decision: whether any current fossil fuel activity qualifies an organisation, or only activity above a stated share of its business (4.5). *Recommendation: any current activity.* Changes: tier 2 counts substantially.

**6. Evidence for tier 3.** Options: (a) appearance in one or more approved published lists of fossil fuel financiers; (b) any documented financing found by look-up. *Recommendation: (a)*, because (b) cannot be applied evenly across institutions. Needed: the lead researcher's view on which lists are acceptable. Changes: tier 3 counts and their defensibility.

**7. Trade associations.** Options: (a) the three alternative criteria of governance, purpose and lobbying record (3.4); (b) the tier of the sector the association represents; (c) a separate line with no tier. *Recommendation: (b) for single-sector associations and (c) for cross-sector federations*, because (a) requires evidence that is not available for most associations. Changes: tiers 1 and 2.

**8. Subsidiaries.** Options: (a) a subsidiary takes its parent's tier; (b) it is classified on its own activity and the parent is recorded. *Recommendation: (b) as the headline with (a) shown as a variant.* Changes: tiers 1 and 2.

**9. Consultancies and law firms.** *Recommendation: classified as business, with no fossil fuel tier, and the gap stated as a limitation.* Changes: the study will under-count indirect ties.

**10. The European Union's own delegation.** Options: (a) reported as a 28th delegation beside the Member States; (b) reported separately from them. *Recommendation: (a) in tables, with totals shown both with and without it.* Changes: EU-wide totals.

**11. Delegates with no stated organisation.** Options: (a) report as unknown; (b) look the person up on public pages and record the employer only. *Recommendation: (a) unless Transparency International confirms that (b) fits its data protection practice;* the number affected at the outset is small. Changes: the size of the unknown row.

**12. The expert's work.** For agreement: who the expert is; that the expert reviews every organisation the model places in a tier and labels a random sample of about 250 others; whether the expert also labels the 50 pilot organisations, so that the pilot gives a first indication of accuracy; and whether a second person can label part of the sample. *Recommendation: yes to each;* without a second person there is no measure of how far two experts would agree. Changes: how firmly the figures can be stated.

**13. Accuracy thresholds.** The values are for the lead researcher to set, before the full run. Starting points, which are this study's proposal and come from no source: at least 90 per cent of business labels correct; at least 95 per cent of tier 1 labels correct; at least 80 per cent of true business and tier cases found. If a threshold is missed, the run is repeated with changed rules or a stronger model and measured on a fresh sample (6.5). Changes: whether the figures can be published at all.

**14. The total used for shares.** Options: (a) all registrations of the delegation, unknowns included, with the range shown; (b) only registrations whose organisation could be classified. *Recommendation: (a)*, because (b) raises every share where unknowns are many. Changes: every percentage.

**15. Tiers for organisations that are not businesses.** Options: (a) tiers apply to business organisations and state-owned enterprises only; (b) a public body, research institute or other organisation with fossil fuel activity can also hold a tier. *Recommendation: (a).* Changes: the fossil fuel-linked count.

**16. Question 1 for all countries.** The brief asks for this count "if feasible". Options: (a) decide after the pilot, on its cost and accuracy; (b) commit now; (c) drop. *Recommendation: (a).* This narrows what the brief asks for until then. Changes: the scope of the publication.

**17. Reporting by relation to the government.** The list records how each delegate relates to the nominating government (section 5). Options: (a) not used; (b) business and fossil fuel-linked delegates are also shown by relation. *Recommendation: (b), as a secondary table,* once the field has been profiled. Changes: one further table.

**18. Naming organisations.** For decision: whether organisations classified as fossil fuel-linked are named in the publication; and whether an organisation is given the chance to respond before it is named. No recommendation is made; this is a matter of Transparency International's practice. Changes: the checks required before publication.

One further point needs agreement but does not change the method. The scope of the literature search has not been agreed. So far, the scope has been sources in English, screened from their titles; the references of the three starting sources were taken without a limit on year, and the catalogue search was limited to works from 2015. A different scope can be set.

## Annex A. Technical requirements

The method in sections 5 to 7 is specified by the following requirements. The full text is in the study's technical requirements document (draft v4, 24 November 2026).

**Research standard.** R1 Rules, sample design and thresholds are written and approved before the full run. R2 Each definition has a cited source or is marked as the study's own. R3 The literature search is documented with counts. R4 Every classification has a source and quotation; every number comes from a generated table; every citation has a page. R5 An expert checks a random sample; error rates and unknowns are reported. R6 Results are shown under stricter and looser definitions. R7 Code, rules, model version, dates and the classified table are kept. R8 Limitations are stated.

**Literature.** L1 A ledger of every source considered, with status and reason. L2 A search log with counts. L3 Sources found from the starting sources, their references, an open scholarly catalogue and web search. L4 Screened-in sources are downloaded unaltered; only what is screened in is fetched; sites' rules for automated tools are respected; candidates are sorted into tiers before screening. L5 One reading record per source, each item with quotation and page; nothing filled from memory. L6 to L8 Text is split into passages and searchable on the researcher's own computer; search quality is tested against questions with known answers. L9 No summaries are stored in place of sources.

**Review and rules.** K1 The review answers eight fixed questions (sectors; evidence for tiers 2 and 3; subsidiaries; trade associations, consultancies and law firms; state-owned enterprises; the EU's own delegation; published lists; how earlier counts identified people). K2 to K4 The rules give, for each category, a definition, indicators, ranked evidence, examples and hard cases; they define confidence and the reasons for unknown; they carry a version number and are approved before use.

**Data.** D1 Original files are never edited. D2 Analysis is at organisation and delegation level. D3 Each dataset has a profile. D4 Cleaning is code with a counted log; row counts reconcile. D5 Inferred values never overwrite. D6 A person on both sheets for one country is counted once, on the Party badge. D7 The EU scope is 28 fixed nominating bodies. D8 One organisation table with a stable identifier and every spelling.

**Classification.** C1 Per organisation. C2 One record with category, reasoning, source, quotation, date, matched body and confidence. C3 Confidence defined by evidence. C4 Unknown always has one of seven reasons. C5 Cheapest step first. C6 A missing affiliation may be looked up on public pages only; employer and source only. C7 No source, no label. C8 A pilot of 50. C9 Results are stored per organisation.

**Evaluation.** V1 Automatic checks on every record. V2 A random 10 per cent classified twice. V3 Known answers from approved lists. V4 An expert sample of about 250, seeded, stratified, including predicted negatives. V5 The expert labels blind. V6 Accuracy per confidence level; precision and recall with confidence intervals; a confusion table. V7 Thresholds agreed beforehand and applied. V8 Every named fossil fuel-linked organisation is checked by a person.

**Analysis and writing.** A1 One command regenerates every table and chart. A2 Count and share, by badge type, with unknowns. A3 Sensitivity versions. W1 to W4 The report is drafted from the rules, logs and tables; a check fails any number without a table or citation without a record; revision is by independent reviewers; standing caveats are always included.

**Proposal and exchanges.** P1 to P9 This proposal; two exchanges with the lead researcher (proposal and report); what she returns is kept unchanged, compared, and its changes of substance logged; her text is checked like any other; a house style derived from Transparency International's publications.

A further set of requirements, for a graph of the data, has been proposed and is not approved. It is not part of this proposal.
